# Defensible Evidence & CISO Planning for Resistance
## SCRMS Annex 3 & Annex 4

---

## What is Defensible Evidence?

Defensible evidence is documented evidence that is so well-collected and preserved that its adequacy and sufficiency are:
- **Irrefutable** — authenticity and integrity make it admissible in court or formal proceedings
- **Legally survivable** — capable of withstanding challenges from auditors, regulators, class action lawsuits, etc.

Defensible evidence should be the **natural byproduct** of secure, compliant, and resilient practices — not manufactured for audits. Evidence demonstrates conformity with the LCS, including validation of material controls and documented risk decisions.

---

## Annex 3: The 6 Evidence Categories

### A3.1 — Governance Evidence
**Objective:** Clearly demonstrate executive leadership oversight and accountability.

Evidence types include:
- Cybersecurity program charter
- Cybersecurity strategy (multi-year roadmap)
- Quarterly Business Review (QBR) briefing materials
- Security steering committee meeting minutes
- Policies and standards (published and version-controlled)

### A3.2 — Risk Evidence
**Objective:** Clearly demonstrate risks were identified and managed.

Evidence types include:
- Risk Management Program (RMP) documentation
- Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM) plan
- Formal risk assessments
- Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)
- Threat intelligence feeds and reports
- Threat catalog (entity-specific)
- Entity-specific threat analysis
- Risk catalog
- Risk register or Plan of Action & Milestones (POA&M)
- Control gap analysis
- Risk acceptance documentation (signed by appropriate authority)

### A3.3 — Security Capability Evidence
**Objective:** Clearly demonstrate appropriately scoped controls exist and operate effectively.

Evidence types include:
- Documented policies, standards, and procedures
- System Security Plan (SSP) or similar documentation
- Secure Baseline Configurations (SBC) for deployed technology assets
- IT Asset Management (ITAM) program documentation
- Identity and Access Management (IAM) program evidence
- Incident Response Plans (IRPs)
- Security event monitoring logs and reports
- Vulnerability and Patch Management Program (VPMP) documentation
- Vulnerability assessment scan results

### A3.4 — Compliance Evidence
**Objective:** Clearly demonstrate conformity with statutory, regulatory, and/or contractual obligations.

Evidence types include:
- Identification of applicable laws, regulations, and contracts with entity-specific obligations
- Evidence of conformity with one or more cybersecurity frameworks (if applicable)
- Results from assessments and audits
- Evidence of how non-conformity is governed (e.g., compensating controls)
- Plan of Action & Milestones (POA&M) to govern non-conformity

### A3.5 — Resilience Evidence
**Objective:** Clearly demonstrate the entity is reasonably capable of recovering from incidents.

Evidence types include:
- Incident response reports (post-incident)
- Evidence of actions taken from Root Cause Analysis (RCA) after incidents
- Incident response exercises (tabletop, functional)
- Disaster recovery test results
- Business continuity exercise reports

### A3.6 — Assurance Evidence
**Objective:** Clearly demonstrate independent validation of the entity's cybersecurity program.

Evidence types include:
- Third-party audits (compliance audits, security assessments)
- Penetration testing reports
- Reports from regulatory inspections

## Annex 4: Planning for Resistance — CISO Defensibility

A CISO must be able to demonstrate both due diligence and due care. The defensible position is:

> *"The entity's leadership was informed of the risk, the controls were recommended, and leadership knowingly accepted the risk."*

The CISO's job is to make that statement **provable** through defensible evidence.

### The Four Critical Questions Investigators Ask
After a significant incident, investigators typically ask:
1. Was risk identified?
2. Was leadership informed?
3. Were reasonable controls recommended?
4. Did leadership knowingly accept the risk?

A CISO should maintain evidence to answer **yes** to each of these with documentation.

---

## A4.1 — Formal Cybersecurity Strategy & Implementation Plan

The first line of defense: a credible plan aligned with industry-recognized secure practices.

**Evidence needed:**
1. Formal cybersecurity strategy (e.g., 3–5 year roadmap)
2. Budget requests to implement the strategy
3. Prioritized objectives to operationalize the strategy
4. Objectives assigned to roles/individuals within the cybersecurity department
5. Status reports on the state of strategy rollout

This demonstrates proactive leadership by showing the CISO:
- Performed due diligence to identify security, compliance and resilience needs
- Leveraged recognized standards for reasonable care
- Identified capability gaps
- Proposed reasonable solutions

---

## A4.2 — Documented Risk Assessments and Business Impact Assessments (BIAs)

Executive leadership cannot reasonably claim ignorance if the CISO clearly quantifies and documents risk in language executives understand.

**Evidence needed:**
1. Formal risk assessments including:
   - Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)
   - Supply Chain Risk Assessments (SCRAs)
2. Business Impact Analysis (BIA)
3. Threat modeling and attack scenarios
4. Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM) plan

**Frame risk in executive language:**
- Financial loss
- Regulatory exposure
- Operational downtime
- Safety impact

---

## A4.3 — Formal Risk Register

The risk register is one of the **strongest pieces of defensible evidence** for a CISO. It must be properly maintained with immutable date/time stamp entries.

**Minimum required fields:**
1. Risk description
2. Likelihood
3. Impact (including non-compliance with statutory/regulatory/contractual obligations)
4. Affected assets
5. Recommended controls and/or compensating controls
6. Estimated remediation cost and timeline
7. Risk owner
8. Risk acceptance status

---

## A4.4 — Incidents Tracking

Incidents are often the catalyst for broader scrutiny. Linking incidents to unfunded capabilities provides CISO justification for budget.

**Evidence needed:**
1. Attempted attacks that were prevented
2. Incidents that occurred (with documentation of response)
3. Phishing campaign results
4. Penetration test findings

---

## A4.5 — Third-Party Assessments

External validation significantly strengthens defensibility. Independent expert recommendations help CISOs justify budget requests and capability prioritization.

**Evidence needed:**
1. Third-party assessment reports
2. Penetration testing reports
3. Compliance audit reports
4. Maturity assessment reports

If executive leadership ignores independent expert advice, the CISO is better protected by clearly stating:
- Capability gaps identified
- Controls recommended
- Risk exposure communicated

---

## A4.6 — Document Decisions (CISO Journal)

A "CISO Journal" serves as a structured log of analysis and decisions — extremely valuable during litigation or investigations. It is a chronological record of the CISO's thoughts, actions, and stakeholder interactions.

**Example:** "Meeting with CEO on [date] where CEO delayed breach detection capability for [reason]."

**In addition to the journal, CISOs should maintain copies of:**
1. Strategy documents
2. Risk assessments
3. Board presentations
4. Budget requests
5. Rejected initiatives
6. Escalation communications

**Note on storage:** Due to the sensitive nature, the CISO may email evidence to their work email so it is capable of being found during eDiscovery if employment ends after an incident.

---

## A4.7 — Reporting Risk to Executive Leadership

CISOs should ensure security, compliance, and resilience topics appear in board reporting.

**Evidence needed:**
1. Quarterly Business Review (QBR) board briefings
2. Risk committee presentations
3. Audit committee presentations
4. Cybersecurity dashboards
5. Executive leadership-focused security program updates

**If executive leadership knowingly exposes the entity to illegal or catastrophic risk, professional options for the CISO include:**
- Escalating to the board audit committee
- Involving internal audit
- Seeking legal counsel
- Resigning with documented concerns
- Whistleblower submissions (e.g., False Claims Act qui tam actions)

---

## A4.8 — Formal Risk Acceptance from Leadership

The goal is **documented executive accountability** — the CISO's role is NOT to own the risk. Escalation should always be documented in writing. If executives refuse funding, the CISO should require written risk acceptance.

**CISO escalation path:**
1. CIO
2. Executive leadership (CFO, COO, CEO)
3. Board-level risk committee
4. Board-level audit committee

**Methods for documenting risk acceptance:**
- **Risk Acceptance Form (RAF)** — signed by one or more executives (note: politically sensitive)
- **Board meeting minutes** — neutral capture method (assumes minutes capture necessary details)
- **Email summary** — clear, concise email summarizing the risk, the request, and the reason for refusal (designed to be found during eDiscovery)

---

## A4.9 — Preserve Evidence of Professional Competence

Maintain documentation showing the CISO acted reasonably with available resources.

**Evidence needed:**
1. Properly scoped policies and standards
2. Security architecture aligned with industry-recognized secure practices
3. Implementation of Secure Baseline Configurations (SBC)
4. Tested and viable incident response capabilities
5. Recurring security training that encourages business-as-usual secure practices
6. Proactive risk management program
7. Proactive vulnerability management program

## Security, Compliance & Resilience Conformity Assessment Program (SCR CAP)

The SCR CAP is the SCF's conformity assessment program for third-party validation of SCRMS implementation. Key features:
- Uses tailored cybersecurity and privacy controls specific to the entity's statutory, regulatory, and contractual obligations
- Uses the SCF as the metaframework — a single assessment can cover multiple frameworks (HIPAA, CMMC, SOC 2, ISO 27001, etc.)
- Assesses controls that reflect real-world requirements (not just "checkbox" compliance)
- Performed by a Third-Party Assessment Organization (3PAO)
